About me

My research is on auditory attention decoding: working out, from a listener's brain activity, which of several people talking at once they are actually trying to follow. A hearing aid has to guess this today from indirect cues such as head orientation or which voice is loudest, and in a crowded room those cues are frequently wrong. Electroencephalography offers a more direct route, because the brain tracks an attended voice more strongly than the ones being ignored. I work on this under the supervision of Prof. Alexander Bertrand and Dr. Simon Geirnaert.

These methods work well in a laboratory and much less well outside one. Published results tend to rest on carefully controlled speech, long uninterrupted recordings and dense arrays of scalp electrodes, while a hearing device has to cope with ordinary conversation, with listeners whose hearing and neural responses differ widely from one another, and with a handful of electrodes hidden around the ear. I work on decoding algorithms that treat that variability as something to be modelled rather than avoided, and that adapt to a new listener without first asking them to sit through a calibration session.

I am a biomedical engineer by training. Before moving to Leuven I held a research fellowship at Politecnico di Torino, working on automated assessment of sleep disorders from overnight recordings, and my master's thesis applied transformer models to vocal signals in Parkinson's disease. I studied in Turin and spent an Erasmus semester at Trinity College Dublin.

Voice classification in Parkinson's disease: a deep learning approach using transformers and error rate metrics

Perrone, B. · Amato, F. · Olmo, G.

Speech changes affect most people with Parkinson's disease, and often appear early. We asked whether the mistakes an automatic transcription system makes on a recording can stand in for a clinical severity score, and paired that measure with transformer models trained across three clinical datasets and two languages.

Full-night comparison of ECG- and PPG-derived measures of cardiac variability for sleep disorder screening

Ciampa, I. · Perrone, B. · et al.

A wrist wearable measures the pulse, not the electrical activity of the heart, and the two are widely assumed to be interchangeable. We compared them over entire nights in fifty people, healthy and with sleep disorders, and worked out which variability measures survive that comparison and which quietly do not.
